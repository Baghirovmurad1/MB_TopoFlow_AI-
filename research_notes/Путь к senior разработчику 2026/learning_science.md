## Q1. What do meta-analyses say about the general learning techniques (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, elaboration, self-explanation, deliberate practice)? Effect sizes.

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing have the strongest and most general evidence (Dunlosky "high utility"; g ≈ 0.5–0.6). Worked examples (g ≈ 0.48) and self-explanation (g ≈ 0.55) are strong, but worked examples help novices most and stop helping as expertise grows (expertise reversal). Interleaving is moderate (g ≈ 0.42) and depends on how similar the categories are. Deliberate practice matters, but it explains much less variance than the "10,000 hours" story says, and the share is especially small in professions (<1%).

### Cited Findings

**Dunlosky et al. 2013 ratings (PSPI)**
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58, reviewed 10 techniques. **High utility:** practice testing, distributed practice. **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. **Low:** summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. **[verified]** — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- Moderate ratings reflected limited evidence, especially in real educational settings, not evidence that the techniques fail. Dunlosky: "By just replacing rereading with delayed retrieval practice, students would benefit." **[verified]** — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm); [Kent State](https://www.kent.edu/node/230016)

**Retrieval practice / testing effect**
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), *Review of Educational Research* 87(3), 659–701, synthesized 118 studies. Practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions, and feedback amplified the effect. **[verified]** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/). The overall effect is commonly reported as g = 0.61 (0.51 vs. restudy) **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]**.
- Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6), meta-analysis of the testing effect: g ≈ 0.50 overall, larger with feedback and recall-type tests **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1037/a0037559](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)
- Pan & Rickard (2018), *Psychological Bulletin*: transfer of test-enhanced learning d ≈ 0.40, stronger when initial retrieval is elaborative or the material aligns with the transfer test **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1037/bul0000151](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)

**Spacing / distributed practice**
- Cepeda et al. (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 839 assessments in 317 experiments: spaced practice beats massed practice. The optimal gap grows with the retention interval **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)
- Cepeda et al. (2008), *Psychological Science*: the optimal gap is roughly 10–20% of the retention interval for ~1-week retention, and the ratio falls to ~5–10% for ~1-year retention **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)

**Interleaving**
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* ("Similarity matters"): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, overall g = 0.42. Math g = 0.34, paintings g = 0.67, and words favored **blocking** (g = −0.39). Interleaving helps most when categories look similar to each other but items within a category vary, which is a discrimination problem. **[verified]** — [Würzburg preprint PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [Frontiers correction noting version differences for math](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02296/full)

**Worked examples, fading, expertise reversal**
- Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), *Educational Psychology Review*, worked examples in mathematics: 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes, average g = 0.48. Adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples produced **lower** performance than worked examples alone. **[verified via abstract summaries]** — [Kent State OAKS record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [Learning & the Brain summary](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218); [DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)
- Expertise reversal effect: techniques that help novices (worked examples, integrated guidance) become redundant or harmful for more knowledgeable learners, who then do better with problem solving. Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38(1) **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4); Kalyuga (2007), *Educational Psychology Review* 19 — [DOI 10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3)
- Fading means moving from full worked example to completion problem to independent problem, gradually removing solution steps. Renkl & Atkinson (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38(1) **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3)

**Self-explanation and elaboration**
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 703–725: 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, ~5,900 participants, random-effects g = 0.55. The effect held when time on task was controlled. **[verified]** — [Gwern-hosted PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)
- Tension: Bisra finds self-explanation is strong on its own, while Barbieri finds that adding prompts to math worked examples hurt. Prompt design and cognitive load likely matter. — [Bisra](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [Barbieri summary](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218)

**Desirable difficulties (Bjork)**
- Bjork & Bjork (2011) separate *performance during learning* from *learning itself*. Spacing, interleaving, testing and generation slow apparent progress but improve long-term retention and transfer. Learners routinely misjudge massed practice and rereading as more effective. **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [Bjork & Bjork 2011 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

**Deliberate practice (Ericsson vs. Macnamara)**
- Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), *Psychological Review* 100(3): accumulated deliberate practice largely explains differences in expert performance (violinists) **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363)
- Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science* 25(8), 88 studies: deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and **<1% in professions**. **[verified]** — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/news/releases/becoming-an-expert-takes-more-than-practice.html); [stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/macnamara-2014-deliberate-practice-and/)
- Critics, Ericsson among them, argue that the meta-analysis mixed in loose definitions of "deliberate practice" and unreliable measures. One reanalysis estimated 29–61% of variance after correcting for attenuation. **[verified as existence of critique]** — [Frontiers commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00751/full); [Hambrick et al. reply, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4101876/)
- Macnamara et al. (2016), sports meta-analysis: deliberate practice explained 18% of variance, and only about 1% among elite athletes **[verified that paper exists; elite figure bibliographic]** — [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-et-al-2016-the-relationship-between-deliberate-practice-and-performance-in-sports-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Macnamara & Maitra (2019), *Royal Society Open Science*, double-blind replication of the 1993 violinist study (n = 39): did **not** replicate the core finding. Practice explained ~26% of variance vs. the 48% originally claimed, and many of the best violinists had practiced less than the "good" group. **[verified]** — [PMC6731745](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745); [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-maitra-2019-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-expert-performance-revisiting-ericsson-krampe-tesch-romer.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Python and software engineering the transferable core is: (1) replace rereading docs or tutorials with retrieval, such as writing code from memory, predicting output, or answering flashcards; (2) space reviews over weeks; (3) interleave problem *types* that are easy to confuse, such as list vs. dict vs. set choices or recursion vs. iteration, rather than doing 20 problems of one type in a row.
- Worked examples are the right default for a true beginner in a new domain (asyncio, SQL query plans, Kubernetes). Switch to unaided problem solving as competence grows, because expertise reversal predicts that copying examples stops helping.
- "Deliberate practice in professions explains <1%" should not be read as "practice doesn't matter for programmers." Professional tasks are poorly defined, practice is hard to measure, and the samples are noisy. What it does mean is that hour-counting alone predicts little, so the quality and type of practice matter more than volume.

### Gaps
- Exact overall g for Adesope 2017 and Rowland 2014 were not re-fetched (paywalled); figures come from prior knowledge.
- No meta-analysis found that measures these techniques specifically on programming outcomes. Effect sizes come from math, science and verbal domains and are inferred to transfer.

## Q2. Programming-specific evidence: Parsons problems, PRIMM, tracing/reading before writing, notional machine, subgoal labels, productive failure, Use-Modify-Create, katas, code review.

### Takeaway
The computing-education literature consistently supports a **read → trace → modify → write** progression. Tracing and explaining skills predict code-writing skill (Lister group). PRIMM and Use-Modify-Create build this into a lesson structure. Parsons problems give similar learning in less time. Subgoal labels help in some studies but replicate inconsistently. Productive failure has meta-analytic support (g = 0.36) but was not tested on programming specifically. Code review transfers knowledge in industry. Evidence for code katas is anecdotal.

### Cited Findings

**Reading and tracing before writing (Lister et al.)**
- Lister et al. (2004), ITiCSE working group, students in 7 countries: many novices were weak at predicting the output of short code and at choosing correct code completions. The authors concluded students have "a fragile grasp of skills that are a prerequisite for problem-solving." **[verified]** — [UTS open-access record](https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/4126); [ITiCSE04 WG PDF](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)
- McCracken et al. (2001), multi-institutional study: first-year students across several universities largely could not complete a simple programming problem, which motivated the Lister follow-up **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1145/572139.572181](https://doi.org/10.1145/572139.572181)
- Lopez, Whalley, Robbins & Lister (2008), ICER: path analysis found tracing and code-explaining ("explain in plain English") skills jointly predicted code-writing performance. Venables, Tan & Lister (2009, ICER) found the same pattern: a student who cannot trace reliably rarely writes code well, though tracing alone is not sufficient **[bibliographic, not re-fetched; the search confirmed the 2009 paper exists]** — [DOI 10.1145/1404520.1404531](https://doi.org/10.1145/1404520.1404531); [Lister VU publication list](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/view/people/Lister=3ARaymond=3A=3A.html)

**PRIMM (Predict–Run–Investigate–Modify–Make)**
- Sentance, Waite & Kallia (2019), *Computer Science Education*: 13 schools, 493 students aged 11–14, 8–12 weeks. PRIMM classes scored higher than control on the post-test, and teachers reported that it helped mixed-ability classes. Grounded in Vygotskian sociocultural theory, with an emphasis on talk and language. **[verified via abstract]** — [QMUL repository](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/57800); [Raspberry Pi PRIMM summary](https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/curriculum/quickreads/11-Pedagogy_Summary_PRIMM_V4_2023.pdf)
- Caveats: the study population is school-age, and the effect size was not retrieved.

**Use–Modify–Create**
- Lee et al. (2011), *ACM Inroads*: a progression where learners use an existing artifact, modify it, and then create their own. This is a design framework, not an RCT-validated intervention **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1145/1929887.1929902](https://doi.org/10.1145/1929887.1929902)

**Parsons problems**
- Ericson, Margulieux & Rick (2017), Koli Calling, and Ericson's Georgia Tech dissertation: across three studies, students solved Parsons problems significantly faster than fixing or writing equivalent code, with the **same pre-to-post learning gains**. Adaptive Parsons problems helped more learners finish. **[verified via GT summary]** — [Georgia Tech news](https://hg.gatech.edu/node/603172); [DOI 10.1145/3141880.3141895](https://doi.org/10.1145/3141880.3141895)
- Hou, Ericson & Wang (ITiCSE 2023), 89 undergraduates: Parsons problems offered as on-demand help improved practice performance and efficiency, with posttest equal to unaided code writing. **[verified]** — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/search/author:%22Ericson,%20Barbara%20Jane%22)
- An 81-student study found Parsons scaffolding cut time, but neither condition showed significant learning gain. ICER 2024 (n = 199 sixth graders): Parsons scaffolding gave higher grades and less time. The evidence is mixed on whether Parsons problems *add* learning or only save time. **[verified]** — [NSF PAR 10510497](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10510497-understanding-effects-using-parsons-problems-scaffold-code-writing-students-varying-cs-self-efficacy-levels); [ICER 2024](https://icer2024.acm.org/details/icer-2024-papers/20/Scaffolding-Novices-Analyzing-When-and-How-Parsons-Problems-Impact-Novice-Programmin)
- Systematic review: Ericson et al. (2022), ITiCSE WG, "Parsons Problems and Beyond" **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1145/3571785.3574127](https://doi.org/10.1145/3571785.3574127)

**Subgoal labels**
- Margulieux, Guzdial & Catrambone (2012), ICER: subgoal-labeled worked examples improved performance and transfer when learning App Inventor **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1145/2361276.2361291](https://doi.org/10.1145/2361276.2361291)
- Morrison, Margulieux & Guzdial (2015), ICER: **mixed** results in a CS1 programming task. Gains did not replicate as expected for students *given* labels. The authors suggest programming may impose different cognitive demands than math or science. **[verified]** — [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/530e13c6-feb5-4b72-844f-9184f5ddbe9d)
- Decker, Margulieux & Morrison (2019), semester-long Java CS1: subgoal students produced higher-quality answers (SOLO taxonomy) on 3 of 4 quizzes. Later work reports lower withdrawal and failure among at-risk students. **[verified]** — [Buffalo ResearchConnect](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/using-the-solo-taxonomy-to-understand-subgoal-labels-effect-in-cs/); [Raspberry Pi PDF on subgoal labels](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)

**Notional machine**
- A notional machine is an idealized model of how a program executes, for example Python's names-bind-to-objects model, the call stack and the heap. Sorva (2013), *ACM TOCE*, reviews notional machines and argues that many novice misconceptions come from a missing or wrong one. The concept goes back to du Boulay (1986) **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1145/2483710.2483713](https://doi.org/10.1145/2483710.2483713)

**Productive failure (Kapur)**
- Sinha & Kapur (2021), *Review of Educational Research*: 53 studies, 166 comparisons. Problem-solving-before-instruction beat instruction-first with g = 0.36 [95% CI 0.20, 0.51]. With high fidelity to productive-failure design principles, g ranged 0.37–0.58. The effect reversed for young learners (grades 2–5) and for domain-general skills. Domains were math and science, **not programming**. **[verified via multiple summaries]** — [UNH teaching hub PDF](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-productive-failure-kapor.pdf); [THE Campus](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/using-productive-failure-activate-deeper-learning); [Futurity](https://futurity.org/?p=2629782)
- Conditions: learners need enough prior knowledge to attempt the problem, and the problem should be challenging but not impossible. Consolidation instruction must follow the attempt. **[verified]** — [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/)
- Tension with worked examples: worked examples (g = 0.48) and productive failure (g = 0.36) look contradictory. Both are reconciled by prior knowledge (expertise reversal) and by outcome type: productive failure targets conceptual understanding, worked examples target procedures. — [Barbieri](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218); [Sinha & Kapur summary](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-productive-failure-kapor.pdf)

**Code review as learning**
- Bacchelli & Bird (2013), ICSE, Microsoft (observations, 17 interviews, survey of 873 programmers and 165 managers, 570 classified comments): defect finding was the main expectation, but actual outcomes were more about **knowledge transfer, team awareness and alternative solutions**. Understanding the change was the core challenge. **[verified]** — [ICSE 2013 page](https://2013.icse-conferences.org/content/expectations-outcomes-and-challenges-modern-code-review.html); [author PDF](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2018-481/readings/codereview.pdf)

**AI assistants and skill formation (2025–2026, highly relevant)**
- Anthropic RCT (Jan 2026), 52 mostly junior Python developers learning the Trio library: the AI-assisted group scored 50% vs. 67% on a no-AI quiz (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01). The gap was largest on debugging questions, and the speed gain was not significant. Learners who asked conceptual questions, or who generated code and then asked for explanations, scored ≥65%. Those who fully delegated or relied on AI for debugging scored <40%. These sub-patterns are small-n (2–7 each) and correlational. **[verified]** — [Anthropic research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/02/ai-coding-skill-formation/)
- Bastani et al. (2025), *PNAS*, ~1,000 high-school math students: unrestricted GPT-4 ("GPT Base") improved practice grades by 48% but cut later exam scores by 17% vs. control. A tutor version with guardrails largely removed the harm. **[verified]** — [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- Prather et al. (2024), ICER, "The Widening Gap": in a CS1 setting, generative AI helped stronger novices but worsened metacognitive difficulties for struggling ones **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1145/3632620.3671116](https://doi.org/10.1145/3632620.3671116)

**Code katas / deliberate practice in coding**
- I found no peer-reviewed controlled study of code katas as a training method. The evidence is practitioner anecdote. See Gaps.

### Inferences — concrete application to Python
- **PRIMM loop for each new Python concept** (comprehensions, generators, decorators, context managers, asyncio):
  1. *Predict* the output of a 10–20 line snippet on paper.
  2. *Run* it and compare.
  3. *Investigate* with questions, by tracing in `pythontutor.com` or `pdb`.
  4. *Modify* it with 1–5 line changes.
  5. *Make* a small new program.
- **Tracing drills**: hand-trace variable tables for loops, recursion and mutable-default-argument bugs. Build a correct notional machine of Python (names vs. objects, mutability, references, scope, the event loop).
- **Explain in plain English**: after reading a function, write a one-line summary of *what* it does, not *how*. This is the Lister "relational" skill that predicts writing ability.
- **Parsons problems** for new syntax and idioms, to get exposure quickly without a blank page.
- **Subgoal-labeled worked examples**: annotate solutions with labels such as "1. parse input, 2. build index dict, 3. iterate & accumulate, 4. format output." Fade them over time.
- **Productive failure for concepts**: try to design, for example, a cache or a rate limiter *before* reading the canonical solution (LRU, token bucket), then study it. Do this only after the basics are solid.
- **AI usage rule (evidence-backed)**: while learning, use AI for conceptual questions and explanations, not code generation or auto-debugging. Attempt first, then ask.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of PRIMM, Use-Modify-Create or Parsons problems with pooled effect sizes was found. Individual studies are small and mostly at CS1 or school level.
- No evidence was found on whether these novice techniques extend to professional-level skills (architecture, distributed systems).
- Code katas: no rigorous studies found.

## Q3. How do expert programmers differ from novices, and what builds senior-level judgment?

### Takeaway
Experts hold larger, semantically organized chunks and "programming plans" (schemas) in long-term memory. This lets them read code at the level of intent rather than syntax and spot plan violations. Senior judgment (design, debugging, tradeoffs) appears to come from accumulated, varied, feedback-rich experience, plus time spent understanding the problem. There is little controlled research on how to train it directly.

### Cited Findings
- McKeithen, Reitman, Rueter & Hirtle (1981), *Cognitive Psychology* 13(3): beginners organized programming keywords (ALGOL W) by everyday-language associations. Experts showed similar organizations based on programming semantics, and intermediates were mixed. Expertise is *better-organized* knowledge in meaningful chunks, not just more knowledge. **[verified]** — [UMich Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/1d548813-cc8c-4d00-bc3b-ef1cbd11ed0d); [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/341973/Knowledge_Organization_and_Skill_Differences_In_Computer_Programmers_1)
- Soloway & Ehrlich (1984), *IEEE TSE*: experts use "programming plans" (stereotypical code patterns such as a running-total loop) and "rules of discourse." When programs violated those conventions, expert performance dropped toward novice level. This is chess-style schema evidence for programming **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1109/TSE.1984.5010283](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1984.5010283)
- Felienne Hermans, *The Programmer's Brain* (Manning, 2021): maps cognitive science onto code reading. Confusion splits into lack of knowledge (long-term memory), lack of information (short-term memory) and lack of processing power (working memory). Recommends flashcards for syntax, chunking practice (briefly study code, reproduce it from memory), "beacons," tracing tables, roles of variables, and deliberately reading code **[bibliographic]** — [Manning book page](https://www.manning.com/books/the-programmers-brain)
- Li, Ko & Zhu (2015), ICSE, "What Makes a Great Software Engineer?": 59 interviews with experienced Microsoft engineers identified 53 attributes. Key ones were being a competent coder, maximizing current value, practicing informed decision-making, enabling others, and continuous learning. The non-technical and decision-making attributes stood out **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [Ko PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Li2015GreatEngineers.pdf)
- Sonnentag (1998), *Journal of Applied Psychology*: in professional software design, high performers spent proportionally more time on problem comprehension and used more feedback processes. Years of experience was only weakly related to performance **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.83.5.703](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.83.5.703)
- Bacchelli & Bird (2013): code review spreads knowledge and alternative solutions across a team, an organic channel for building judgment. — [ICSE 2013](https://2013.icse-conferences.org/content/expectations-outcomes-and-challenges-modern-code-review.html)
- In the 2026 Anthropic RCT, the largest AI-induced deficit was in **debugging**, the skill most tied to senior judgment and oversight of AI-generated code. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)

### Inferences — concrete application
- **Building chunks and plans**: read a lot of high-quality Python, such as the stdlib (`collections`, `functools`), `requests` and CPython source. Name the plan each block implements. Do Hermans-style "read for 2 min, reconstruct from memory" exercises.
- **Senior judgment as feedback-rich deliberate practice**:
  - write design docs or ADRs *before* coding, then compare with the outcome later (a prediction-plus-feedback loop);
  - review others' PRs and seek reviews of your own;
  - write postmortems or debugging journals ("hypothesis → test → result");
  - study real system designs and incident reports;
  - pair with seniors.

  These combine retrieval, elaboration and feedback, which are the active ingredients from Q1.
- Following Sonnentag, deliberately invest more time in problem comprehension before solution design.
- Since debugging is what AI erodes most, practice debugging deliberately without AI: reproduce, bisect, form hypotheses.

### Gaps
- No controlled studies were found on *training* system design or architecture judgment. The evidence is descriptive (interviews, expert-novice comparisons).
- Ericsson's own claims about software expertise were not specifically located.

## Q4. Practical tools: Anki/FSRS for programming, Feynman technique, desirable difficulties.

### Takeaway
Spaced-retrieval flashcards are well supported for declarative knowledge such as syntax, APIs, concepts and complexity facts. Practitioners (Sivers, Nielsen) report large gains for programming, but stress atomic cards and pairing with real coding, because cards don't create procedural skill. FSRS is the current best-performing open scheduler on benchmarks, though the benchmark is run by its developers. The "Feynman technique" has no direct trials but rests on well-supported mechanisms: self-explanation (g = 0.55), learning by teaching, and the illusion of explanatory depth.

### Cited Findings
- Derek Sivers, "Memorizing a programming language using spaced repetition software": after a year, he called it "the most helpful learning technique I've found in 14 years of computer programming." Motivated by forgetting features he had tried only once. **[verified]** — [sive.rs/srs](https://sive.rs/srs)
- Michael Nielsen, "Augmenting Long-term Memory" (2018) **[verified]** — [augmentingcognition.com](http://augmentingcognition.com/ltm.html):
  - Keep questions atomic. Split failing questions into sub-questions, as with his Unix `ln -s` argument-order example.
  - Daily review takes ~15–20 min, and a review takes ~8 s. An average card costs about 4–7 minutes of total review over 20 years. Rule of thumb: memorize if a fact is worth about 10 minutes of future time.
  - For APIs: don't memorize before using. Start from a working example, make 1–5 line tweaks, and Ankify only central or reused items. Ankify from your own code.
  - Declarative recall doesn't automatically become skill, so use the material in real work.
  - Add 2–3 cards per idea and avoid orphans. Rewrite yes/no questions.
  - He did not test Anki for problem solving.
- FSRS: open-spaced-repetition benchmark reports FSRS-5 outperforms "SM-2 trainable" in ~97.4% of user collections (forum citation of benchmark). SM-2 itself does not output probabilities, so the comparison uses an augmented SM-2. SuperMemo disputes FSRS comparisons (vendor claim). **[verified as reported; benchmark table not fetched]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fsrs-vs-sm2-in-2-seperate-profiles/52171); [srsbench CRAN README](https://ftp.fau.de/cran/web/packages/srsbench/readme/README.html); [SuperMemo blog](https://supermemo.com/en/blog/supermemo-is-better-than-fsrs-by-far); [FSRS author's notes](https://l-m-sherlock.notion.site/What-are-the-main-differences-between-SM-2-and-FSRS-135c250163a180ca9029ea460136254c)
- Desirable difficulties: spacing, interleaving, retrieval and generation feel harder and *look* worse during practice, but improve retention and transfer. Learners misread fluency as learning. — [Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf) **[bibliographic]**
- Mechanisms behind "Feynman technique" (explain simply, find gaps, refine):
  - Self-explanation g = 0.55 — [Bisra 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
  - The illusion of explanatory depth: people overestimate how well they understand mechanisms until asked to explain them. Rozenblit & Keil (2002) **[bibliographic]** — [DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1)
  - Learning by teaching, i.e. actually explaining to an audience, beyond just expecting to teach: Fiorella & Mayer (2014) **[bibliographic, not re-fetched]** — [DOI 10.1016/j.cedpsych.2014.01.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2014.01.001)
- Generic Anki-for-coders guides (freeCodeCamp, Smashing Magazine) are practitioner sources, not evidence. — [freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/use-spaced-repetition-with-anki-to-learn-to-code-faster-7c334d448c3c); [Smashing](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/08/mastering-a-programming-language-using-spaced-repetition/)

### Inferences — best practices for Python/SE cards
- **Good cards**:
  - "Output of `[x*2 for x in range(3) if x]`?" (prediction or tracing card)
  - "Why is `def f(a=[])` dangerous?"
  - "Big-O of `x in list` vs `x in set`?"
  - "When choose `asyncio` vs threads vs processes?" (tradeoff card)
  - "What does `functools.lru_cache` require of arguments?"
  - "CAP: what's sacrificed under partition in a CP system?"
- **Bad cards**: entire functions to memorize, or vague "explain microservices."
- Use FSRS in Anki at ~0.9 desired retention, spend 15–20 min a day, and source cards from your own bugs, code reviews and docs you actually used, per Nielsen.
- Pair cards with doing: cards for declarative facts, and katas, projects and PRIMM for procedures.
- "Feynman" in practice: write a short explanation, blog post or ADR of a concept for a junior reader, then check it against the source. This is self-explanation plus a test of understanding.

### Gaps
- No controlled trial of Anki/SRS specifically on programming skill outcomes was found. The evidence is general SRS evidence plus practitioner reports.
- The FSRS benchmark log-loss tables were not fetched. The 97.4% figure is via forum.
- No direct study of the "Feynman technique" as a named method exists to my knowledge.

## Q5. How many hours to reach competence or proficiency in programming?

### Takeaway
There is no rigorous data mapping hours to programming competence or seniority. The "10,000 hours" figure is a popularization of Ericsson that his own replication critics weaken. Bootcamps (~3–6 months full-time) get roughly 60–80% of graduates into entry-level jobs, but outcome data are self-reported or vendor-audited. Seniority is typically described in years of varied experience rather than hours.

### Cited Findings
- Accumulated practice explains <1% of performance variance in professions, and ~26% in the violinist replication (vs. 48% claimed), so hour counts are weak predictors. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/news/releases/becoming-an-expert-takes-more-than-practice.html); [Macnamara & Maitra 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745)
- In the replication, groups had roughly 6,000 vs. ~11,000 hours by age 20 (Classic FM), or "good" 9,844 vs. "best" 8,224 hours, not significant (Scienceblog). These secondary sources conflict. **[conflicting]** — [Classic FM](https://classicfm.com/music-news/10-000-hours-practice-doesnt-make-perfect-study); [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-maitra-2019-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-expert-performance-revisiting-ericsson-krampe-tesch-romer.pdf)
- Bootcamps typically run 3–6 months full-time or 6–12 months part-time. CIRR-audited programs report about 60–78% in-field employment within 180 days, with median starting salaries of ~$63–71K (secondary aggregators, 2026). Course Report's 2025 alumni survey reports 79% employed full-time. Methodologies differ and the numbers are not apples-to-apples. **[verified as reported; low-quality secondary sources]** — [Course Report on CIRR](https://www.coursereport.com/blog/announcing-cirr-standardized-job-placement-data-from-coding-bootcamps); [Rockstar Developer University stats](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/coding-bootcamp-statistics/); [BestColleges CIRR list](https://www.bestcolleges.com/?p=847)
- Peter Norvig, "Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years": an argument from expertise research (about ten years or 10,000 hours in many domains) that real mastery takes about a decade of deliberate, varied practice. This is an opinion essay, not data **[bibliographic]** — [norvig.com/21-days.html](https://norvig.com/21-days.html)
- McCracken et al. (2001) and Lister et al. (2004): after a full first university programming course, many students still could not reliably write or even trace simple programs. A single course of instruction (~100+ hours) produces fragile competence. — [Lister UTS](https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/4126); [McCracken DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/572139.572181) **[McCracken bibliographic]**

### Inferences
- A rough planning model, as an inference rather than an evidence-based estimate:
  - fragile basic competence after ~100–200 focused hours (one CS1-equivalent);
  - junior-employable after ~800–1,500 hours of project-based work (bootcamp-equivalent full-time ≈ 500–1,000+ instructional hours plus self-study);
  - senior judgment typically after several years (~5+) of varied production experience with feedback (reviews, incidents, design ownership).
- Given the Macnamara findings, *how* hours are spent (retrieval, feedback, increasing difficulty, varied problems, reading expert code) matters more than the count.
- Heavy AI delegation while learning may *lower* the skill gained per hour (Anthropic d ≈ 0.74 deficit; Bastani −17%), so hours spent delegating should not count as learning hours. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found linking cumulative hours to programmer proficiency level or promotion to senior. CIRR raw reports and university credit-hour data were not retrieved.
- Bootcamp outcome statistics come from aggregator or marketing-adjacent sites. Primary CIRR reports were not fetched.
- No data were found on time-to-senior from industry career-ladder studies (e.g., levels.fyi, Stack Overflow survey years-of-experience vs. title). This could be covered by another researcher.
